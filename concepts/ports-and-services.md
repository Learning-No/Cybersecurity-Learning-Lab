<article class="learning-page"><a class="back-link" href="{{ '/concepts/' | relative_url }}">← Concepts library</a><p class="eyebrow">CONCEPT 06 / NETWORKING</p><h1>Ports & services: <em>where applications listen</em></h1><p class="lead">A port is a transport-layer endpoint number used with protocols such as TCP or UDP. A service may listen on a port, but the port number alone does not prove which software is running or whether a weakness exists.</p>
<div class="diagram-panel"><div class="port-diagram"><div class="flow-node">Device IP<small>192.0.2.10 (documentation example)</small></div><div class="port-branches"><div class="port-branch"><span>TCP · 443</span><b>HTTPS service</b><small>Encrypted web traffic when correctly configured</small></div><div class="port-branch"><span>TCP · 22</span><b>SSH service</b><small>Remote administration; access should be restricted</small></div><div class="port-branch"><span>UDP · 53</span><b>DNS query</b><small>DNS commonly uses UDP; TCP is also used</small></div></div></div><p class="diagram-caption">Illustrative examples only. Services can use non-default ports, and one port number can be used by different protocols.</p></div>
<h2>Important distinctions</h2><div class="definition-list"><details><summary>Port number</summary><p>A number in the transport header that helps deliver data to the right endpoint. TCP and UDP have separate port spaces.</p></details><details><summary>Listening service</summary><p>An application or process accepting connections or datagrams on a local address and port.</p></details><details><summary>Reachability</summary><p>Whether a client can reach the service depends on binding address, routing, firewalls, network policy, and other controls.</p></details><details><summary>Vulnerability</summary><p>A weakness requires evidence about the software, version, configuration, and impact. A port being open is not itself a vulnerability.</p></details></div>
<h2>Safe service-review process</h2><ol class="process-list"><li>Confirm asset ownership and written scope.</li><li>Start with local service inventory on your own machine or lab VM.</li><li>Record address, transport protocol, port, expected service, owner, and business purpose.</li><li>Compare the observation with approved documentation and intended exposure.</li><li>Ask the owner about unknown services; do not assume an alert is a confirmed finding.</li><li>Recommend restricting unnecessary services and retest after an approved change.</li></ol>
<h2>Open-source tools for your own lab</h2><div class="mini-tool-grid"><article><h3>Linux · <code>ss</code></h3><p>Inspect local sockets and listening services.</p><pre><code>ss -lnt
ss -lnu</code></pre><p>These examples list local TCP and UDP listening sockets; they do not scan remote systems.</p></article><article><h3>Windows</h3><p>Use Resource Monitor or built-in PowerShell networking cmdlets to inspect local listening endpoints. Read documentation before running commands on managed devices.</p><a href="https://learn.microsoft.com/en-us/powershell/module/nettcpip/" target="_blank" rel="noopener">PowerShell NetTCPIP docs ↗</a></article><article><h3>Nmap · authorized inventory</h3><p>Nmap can help document services in a deliberately configured lab. Learn the manual and lab rules first; do not probe public or third-party targets without explicit authorization.</p><a href="https://nmap.org/book/man.html" target="_blank" rel="noopener">Nmap reference ↗</a></article></div>
<h2>Practice task</h2><p>On your own lab machine, compare the services you expect to be running with the local listener list. Write down the purpose of each service and a question for the system owner for any unexpected entry. Do not disable services on a managed or production system without approval.</p><p class="resource-links"><a href="https://man7.org/linux/man-pages/man8/ss.8.html" target="_blank" rel="noopener">ss manual ↗</a><a href="{{ '/tools/' | relative_url }}">More open-source tools ↗</a></p></article>
