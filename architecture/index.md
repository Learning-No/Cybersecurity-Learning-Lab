<article class="learning-page"><p class="eyebrow">THINK LIKE A SECURITY ARCHITECT</p><h1>Start with the system, <em>not the tool.</em></h1><p class="lead">Security architecture is the practice of understanding what a system must do, where trust changes, what could go wrong, and which controls reduce risk without breaking the business.</p>
<h2>Architecture review process</h2><div class="process-cards"><article><span>01</span><h3>Understand the mission</h3><p>Who uses the system? What business outcome does it support? What data is sensitive, and what availability is required?</p></article><article><span>02</span><h3>Draw the data flow</h3><p>Show users, devices, applications, APIs, data stores, external services, identity providers, and logs.</p></article><article><span>03</span><h3>Mark trust boundaries</h3><p>Identify where internet, user, service, admin, cloud, and data-zone boundaries are crossed. Treat each crossing as a design question.</p></article><article><span>04</span><h3>Model threats</h3><p>Ask what could be spoofed, tampered with, disclosed, disrupted, or misused. Use STRIDE as a prompt—not as a substitute for context.</p></article><article><span>05</span><h3>Select controls</h3><p>Apply least privilege, strong authentication, segmentation, secure defaults, input validation, encryption, backups, and useful monitoring.</p></article><article><span>06</span><h3>Test assumptions</h3><p>Define abuse cases, verification steps, owners, success criteria, and a safe rollback plan.</p></article><article><span>07</span><h3>Record trade-offs</h3><p>Document decisions, rejected options, operational cost, residual risk, and who accepts it.</p></article></div>
<h2>Diagram: a simple web application</h2><div class="diagram-panel"><div class="architecture-diagram"><div class="arch-zone"><b>Untrusted zone</b><div class="flow-node">User / browser</div></div><div class="flow-arrow">→ TLS</div><div class="arch-zone"><b>Application zone</b><div class="flow-node">Web app / API<small>Auth · validation · rate limits</small></div><div class="flow-arrow">↓ least-privilege identity</div><div class="flow-node">Database<small>Private access · encryption · backups</small></div></div><div class="arch-zone"><b>Operations</b><div class="flow-node">Logs / alerts<small>Restricted access · retention</small></div><div class="flow-node">Admin identity<small>MFA · separate privileges</small></div></div></div><p class="diagram-caption">Illustrative architecture only. Real designs need business context, failure handling, identity boundaries, deployment details, and threat modelling.</p></div>
<h2>Questions an architect asks</h2><ul><li>What is the most valuable data or business process?</li><li>Which components are internet-facing, and why?</li><li>What identity does each service use, and what can it access?</li><li>Where are secrets stored, rotated, and audited?</li><li>What happens if a dependency, identity provider, or region fails?</li><li>Can the team detect misuse and restore service from a known-good backup?</li><li>Who owns each control, and how is it verified?</li></ul>
<h2>Practice project</h2><p>Design a fictional student portal. Create a data-flow diagram, mark trust boundaries, write five threats, map each threat to a control, and explain one trade-off. Use no real student data or real production infrastructure.</p><p class="resource-links"><a href="https://owasp.org/www-project-threat-modeling/" target="_blank" rel="noopener">OWASP Threat Modeling ↗</a><a href="https://csrc.nist.gov/publications" target="_blank" rel="noopener">NIST publications ↗</a><a href="https://www.cisa.gov/securebydesign" target="_blank" rel="noopener">CISA Secure by Design ↗</a></p></article>
